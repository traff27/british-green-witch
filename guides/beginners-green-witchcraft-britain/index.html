A beginner’s guide to green witchcraft in Britain

Last updated: 23 November 2026.

The short answer. Green witchcraft is a nature-based practice built on paying close attention to the plants, places and seasons where you live, and marking the turning year in small, personal ways. You do not need to buy anything, join anything or be Wiccan. Start with a notebook, one plant you know well and one regular walk or candle. Learn plant safety and the law before you pick anything.

What “green witch” means, and what it does not

“Green witch” is a modern term, widely used since the 1990s, for someone whose craft centres on the natural world: plants, trees, weather, the Moon and the seasons. It overlaps with older and newer labels such as hedge witch, kitchen witch and cottage witch, and nobody owns any of them. In practice a green witch might keep a herb pot, know the hedgerow by month, cook with the seasons, mark the solstices, and use simple rituals to notice and give thanks.

It helps to be clear about a few things it is not:

  • It is not the same as Wicca. Wicca is a specific modern religion that became public in England in the 1950s through Gerald Gardner and others. Some green witches are Wiccan; many are not, and many follow no religion at all.
  • It is not an unbroken ancient tradition. British history is full of real folk magic, cunning folk and seasonal custom, and green witches draw on all of it. But nobody can honestly claim a secret line of green witches going back to the Druids. Being honest about what is old and what is modern makes the practice stronger, not weaker.
  • It is not herbal medicine. Knowing plants well is part of the craft. Treating illness is a job for the NHS, a pharmacist or a properly qualified practitioner.
  • It is not an aesthetic you have to buy. Cloaks, cauldrons and crystal kits are optional. Attention is not.

Start where you are

You do not need a cottage, a garden or a wood. Most people in Britain live in towns and cities, and green witchcraft works in a rented flat as well as in the countryside.

  • A windowsill. Grow two or three culinary herbs, such as thyme, rosemary, mint or chives. You will learn how plants respond to light and season, and you can cook with what you grow.
  • A local green space. A park, churchyard, canal towpath or cemetery has trees, birds and wild plants that change through the year. Old churchyards often have yews, which have rich folklore.
  • Your daily route. The walk to the bus or the school gate passes street trees, verges and gardens. Watch them through the year.
  • The sky. Even in a city you can follow the Moon’s phases and the changing times of sunrise and sunset.

A first month, week by week

This is a gentle plan for your first four weeks. Each step takes ten to twenty minutes a few times a week. It needs a notebook, a pen and, if you like, a candle in a safe holder.

  1. Week 1: Find your sit spot and start a notebook

    Choose one place you can visit often: a bench in the park, a corner of the garden, an allotment, or simply a window that looks out at a tree. Spend ten minutes there on three or four days this week, without your phone. Write down what you notice: the light, the weather, birds, what is in flower, what has changed since last time. Date every entry. This notebook is the start of your own book of the year, and it will be more useful to you than anything you can buy.

  2. Week 2: Get to know one plant properly

    Pick one plant you can visit regularly and that is hard to confuse with anything else: bramble, holly, an oak, or a pot of rosemary or thyme on the windowsill. Learn its common and Latin names, how it looks in each season, the folklore attached to it and what can go wrong with it. Draw it, even badly. The point is not to use it yet, but to know it the way you know a neighbour. Do not eat, drink, burn or put on your skin any wild plant this week.

  3. Week 3: Add one small weekly practice

    Choose one small, repeatable act and do it once this week. It might be lighting a candle at the new or full moon (see the 2027 UK moon calendar) and saying a few words of intention or thanks; a short walk at dusk; or a small shelf or windowsill where you put one seasonal find, such as a conker, a feather or a sprig of holly, and change it when the season moves on. Keep it short enough that you will actually repeat it. Use a heat-proof holder, keep candles away from curtains and never leave one burning unattended.

  4. Week 4: Mark the season and look back

    Find out which festival of the Wheel of the Year comes next (the Wheel of the Year dates are worked out for the UK) and mark it in a way that suits you: a seasonal meal, a walk to high ground, a candle in the window. Then read back through your notebook. What did you notice that you would have missed a month ago? Choose one thing to learn next: a second plant, the names of the birds at your sit spot, or the next moon.

After the first month, keep going at the same pace. A year of short, regular notes about the same few places will teach you more than any shelf of books, including ours.

The year as it really is in Britain

Much of what is written about green witchcraft online comes from North America, where the plants, seasons and dates are different. Here is a rough outline of the British year. Timings vary by several weeks between Cornwall and Caithness, and between a sheltered town and an exposed hill.

  • Winter (December to February). Holly, ivy and yew are the evergreens of the season. Hazel catkins appear from January, snowdrops in late January and February, and gorse flowers in almost every month (hence the saying “when gorse is out of bloom, kissing’s out of fashion”). Festivals: Yule and Imbolc.
  • Spring (March to May). Blackthorn blossoms white on bare branches in March and April, primroses fill banks, bluebells carpet woods from around April into May, and hawthorn, the “may”, flowers in May. Festivals: Ostara and Beltane.
  • Summer (June to August). Elderflower from late May into June, dog roses in the hedges, meadowsweet by ditches and streams, and yarrow and St John’s wort around midsummer. Blackberries begin to ripen from late July. Festivals: Litha and Lammas.
  • Autumn (September to November). Blackberries, rosehips, haws, sloes, elderberries and rowan berries; conkers and acorns; ivy flowers feeding the last insects. Folklore says not to pick blackberries after Michaelmas, when the Devil spoils them. Festivals: Mabon and Samhain.

The Wheel of the Year page gives the festival dates for 2026 and 2027 in UK time, and the 2027 moon calendar lists every new and full moon.

Plant safety: read this first

Some of Britain’s most poisonous plants grow in ordinary hedges, verges and gardens, and several look like harmless ones.

  • Never eat, drink, burn or put on your skin any wild plant unless you are completely certain what it is, from more than one reliable source, and know that it is safe for you. If in doubt, leave it out.
  • Know the dangerous look-alikes. Hemlock and hemlock water-dropwort resemble cow parsley and other harmless members of the carrot family, and can kill. Foxglove leaves have been mistaken for comfrey. Yew is poisonous in almost every part, and so are the berries of lords-and-ladies and deadly nightshade. Wild mushrooms need expert identification; the death cap causes most fatal mushroom poisonings.
  • Some plants harm the skin. Giant hogweed sap can cause severe burns and blisters in sunlight. Wear gloves and wash your hands after handling plants you do not know.
  • Take extra care with children, pets, pregnancy and medicines. Many herbs interact with prescription drugs or are unsuitable in pregnancy. Ask a pharmacist or your GP before taking any herb, even a culinary one, in more than food amounts.
  • Do not burn plants you have not identified. Smoke from any source irritates the lungs, and some plants are harmful when burned. Keep rooms ventilated if you use incense or dried herbs.

If you think someone has eaten something poisonous, call NHS 111 for advice, or 999 if they are seriously unwell, drowsy, having a fit or struggling to breathe. Take the plant, or a photo of it, with you.

Foraging, ethics and the law in brief

A green witch should know the law as well as the folklore. In brief, and not as legal advice:

  • Uprooting. Under section 13 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence in Great Britain to uproot any wild plant without the permission of the landowner or occupier. That includes digging up bluebell bulbs, primroses or ferns. Some rare species are protected from picking altogether. Northern Ireland has similar rules under the Wildlife (Northern Ireland) Order 1985.
  • Picking. In England and Wales, section 4(3) of the Theft Act 1968 says that picking mushrooms, flowers, fruit or foliage from a plant growing wild is not theft, unless you do it for reward, for sale or for another commercial purpose. That is not a right of access: you still need to be somewhere you are allowed to be.
  • Scotland. The Scottish Outdoor Access Code sets out responsible access, including picking small amounts of wild berries and mushrooms for your own use.
  • Special places. Nature reserves, Sites of Special Scientific Interest, National Trust land and many parks have byelaws or rules that ban picking. Check the signs and the site’s own website.

Beyond the law, follow the forager’s manners that the Woodland Trust and others recommend: take only a little of anything plentiful, never the first or the last you see, leave plenty for wildlife, and do not trample. At ancient sites and stone circles, leave nothing behind: candles, ribbons and offerings can damage stones and plants and spoil the place for others.

What you do not need to buy

Witchcraft shops and social media can make it seem as if you need a starter kit. You do not. In your first year you need:

  • a notebook and pen
  • a good field guide to British wild plants, or the free identification resources of the Woodland Trust, Plantlife and the Botanical Society of Britain and Ireland
  • a candle and a safe, heat-proof holder, if you want one
  • a few pots, compost and seeds for the windowsill

Everything else is optional. If you do want tools later, a kitchen knife, a bowl from a charity shop and a stone from a beach you love (where taking one is allowed) will serve as well as anything bought new.

A word on health and wellbeing

Many people find that time outdoors, seasonal ritual and quiet attention help them feel calmer and more connected. That is a real benefit, but these practices are not a treatment for physical or mental illness. Keep seeing your GP, and if you are struggling, talk to someone: Samaritans can be reached free, day or night, on 116 123. Our book Witchcraft & Mental Health is written to sit alongside NHS care, never instead of it.

Questions

Do I need to be Wiccan to be a green witch?

No. Wicca is a specific modern religion, made public in England in the 1950s. Green witchcraft is a nature-based practice that many people follow without any religion at all, and others combine with Wicca, Druidry, Christianity or their own beliefs.

Can I practise green witchcraft in a flat?

Yes. A windowsill of herbs, a sit spot in a local park, a seasonal shelf and a notebook are a complete practice. Paying attention to the year where you are matters far more than having a garden.

Is it legal to forage in the UK?

Picking small amounts of wild fruit, flowers, leaves and fungi for your own use is generally lawful, but uprooting any wild plant without the landowner’s permission is an offence in Great Britain, some species are fully protected, and many nature reserves ban picking. In Scotland, the Outdoor Access Code applies. This is a summary, not legal advice.

Do I need to be initiated or join a coven?

No. Most green witches practise alone. Some traditions, such as initiatory Wicca, do have covens and initiation, but you do not need anyone’s permission to pay attention to the land and the seasons.
