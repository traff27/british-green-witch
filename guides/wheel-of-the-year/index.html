Last updated: 23 November 2026. All times are UK clock time: GMT in winter, BST in summer.

Solstices and equinoxes in 2026 and 2027 (UK time)

Four festivals of the Wheel are fixed by the Sun itself. The times below are the exact astronomical moments, given in the clock time used in the UK on that day.

Times are from timeanddate.com’s London table of equinoxes and solstices and agree with the US Naval Observatory to within a minute. The Naval Observatory rounds to the nearest minute, so it shows some 2027 moments a minute later (for example 20:25 rather than 20:24 for the March equinox). The difference is a matter of seconds.

British Summer Time runs from 01:00 GMT on Sunday 28 March to 02:00 BST on Sunday 31 October in 2027, which is why the summer solstice and the autumn equinox are given in BST.

Imbolc, Beltane, Lammas and Samhain: traditional and astronomical dates

The other four festivals, often called the cross-quarter days, fall roughly halfway between a solstice and an equinox. They are usually kept on fixed calendar dates that go back to the old Gaelic and English farming year. Each traditionally begins at sunset the evening before.

Which date should you use? Whichever suits you. The traditional date is what the old festivals actually used, and it is what most people mean by “Beltane” or “Samhain”. The astronomical midpoint is the moment the Sun reaches 315°, 45°, 135° or 225° along its yearly path, exactly halfway between the neighbouring solstice and equinox. Because the seasons are not quite equal in length, that moment lands a few days after the traditional date. Some witches keep both: the traditional date for a gathering, and the astronomical moment for something quieter. Plenty of people simply use the nearest weekend, and that is fine too.

The astronomical midpoints were calculated for The British Green Witch’s Almanac 2027 from NASA JPL’s DE440 planetary ephemeris, the same data the solstice and equinox times rest on.

What the Wheel of the Year is, and how old it is

The Wheel of the Year is the cycle of eight seasonal festivals kept by most modern Pagans, Wiccans and witches. Four are solar: the two solstices and the two equinoxes. Four are seasonal: the cross-quarter days that once marked the start of winter, spring, summer and harvest.

It is worth being straightforward about its history, because a lot of what is written online is not. The festivals themselves are old. Samhain, Imbolc, Beltane and Lughnasadh are named in early medieval Irish literature. Lammas, Candlemas, Midsummer and Christmas-tide were kept in Britain for centuries, and monuments such as Stonehenge, Newgrange and Maeshowe line up with the midsummer or midwinter Sun, which tells us people were watching the solstices thousands of years ago.

What is modern is the wheel itself. Putting eight festivals together at even intervals, and treating them as one connected cycle, was the work of English Pagans in the 1950s and 1960s, notably Gerald Gardner’s Wiccan circles and Ross Nichols’s Druid order. Several of the names now in common use were added later still: “Ostara”, “Litha” and “Mabon” were adopted for the equinoxes and midsummer in the 1970s. None of that makes the Wheel less meaningful. It is a modern frame around a very old picture, and it fits the British year well.

For a careful history, the historian Ronald Hutton’s The Stations of the Sun (1996), on the ritual year in Britain, and The Triumph of the Moon (1999), on modern Pagan witchcraft, are the standard books.

The eight festivals, one by one

The Wheel has no fixed starting point. Many witches begin the year at Samhain, the old Gaelic start of winter; others begin at Yule, when the light starts to return, or simply on 1 January. Here they are in calendar order from the next one due.

Yule: the winter solstice (21 December 2026; 22 December 2027)

The shortest day and longest night. In Shetland the Sun is up for less than six hours. The name comes from the Old English Gēola, which the monk Bede recorded in the eighth century as the name for the midwinter season. Evergreens brought indoors, the Yule log and feasting at midwinter all have long histories in Britain, though many details of “traditional” Yule customs were shaped or revived by the Victorians. A simple way to mark it is to sit with a candle in the dark on the longest night, then notice the first evening that feels lighter.

Imbolc (1 February)

The first stirring of spring: snowdrops, lengthening days and, on farms, the start of lambing. Imbolc was an Irish festival closely tied to St Brigid, whose feast day is 1 February; the meaning of the name itself is debated. In Britain the following day, 2 February, was Candlemas, when candles were blessed in church and weather lore was made (“If Candlemas Day be fair and bright, winter will have another flight”). A good Imbolc practice is a clean-out of the house or a first look at the seed packets.

Ostara: the spring equinox (20 March 2026 and 2027)

Day and night are close to equal and the light is now gaining fast. The name Ostara is a nineteenth-century reconstruction by the folklorist Jacob Grimm, based on Bede’s mention of an English goddess, Eostre, whose month gave its name to Easter. There is no record of an ancient English festival at the equinox itself, so this is one of the most modern of the eight. It is a natural time for sowing, early walks and noticing blackthorn blossom in the hedges.

Beltane (1 May)

The Gaelic festival of the start of summer, when cattle were driven between fires for protection before going out to summer pasture. In England, May Day had its own customs: maypoles, May garlands and gathering dew at dawn on May morning. Some survive, such as the ’Obby ’Oss in Padstow on May Day. Beltane is a time for flowers, hawthorn blossom (the “may”) and being outdoors.

Litha: the summer solstice (21 June 2026 and 2027)

The longest day. “Litha” comes from Bede’s Old English names for June and July, Ǣrra Līþa and Æfterra Līþa, “before” and “after” Litha. Traditional English Midsummer was usually kept on St John’s Eve, 23 June, with bonfires, and Midsummer Day was 24 June. Thousands gather at Stonehenge to watch the sunrise; English Heritage publishes access arrangements each year. A quiet alternative is to watch the sunset from the highest ground near you.

Lughnasadh or Lammas (1 August)

The first harvest. Lammas comes from the Old English hlāfmæsse, “loaf mass”, when bread from the first wheat was blessed in church. Lughnasadh is the Irish festival named after the god Lugh, with fairs, gatherings and hill-top assemblies. It is a festival of bread, early blackberries and gratitude for what has grown.

Mabon: the autumn equinox (23 September 2026 and 2027)

Day and night are equal again, and the dark half of the year begins. The name Mabon, from a figure in Welsh mythology, was attached to the equinox in the 1970s. Older British customs at this time belong to the end of harvest: harvest home suppers, Michaelmas on 29 September, and the church harvest festival, which was popularised by the Cornish vicar Robert Hawker in 1843. It is a good time to gather hips and haws, save seed and give thanks.

Samhain (31 October to 1 November)

The Gaelic start of winter, and for many modern witches the new year. In Wales the same night is Nos Galan Gaeaf, the eve of the winter calends. Samhain has long been linked with the dead and with the thinning of the boundary between worlds, a theme carried into Hallowe’en and the Christian feasts of All Saints and All Souls. A simple practice is to light a candle for those who have died and to set out their names or photographs.

Why other websites give different dates

If you compare festival dates online, you will find disagreements, especially for Yule 2027. There are three usual reasons.

  • Time zones. The 2027 winter solstice is at 02:42 GMT on 22 December, but that is 21:42 on 21 December in New York. Many popular Pagan sites are American and give the American date.
  • Rounding. Different sources round to the nearest minute or cut off the seconds, so times can differ by one minute.
  • Traditional versus astronomical cross-quarter dates. A site that gives Beltane as 5 May is using the astronomical midpoint; one that gives 1 May is using the traditional date.

The dates on this page are worked out for the UK, in UK clock time, and checked against published astronomical sources. They match the 2027 UK moon calendar and the Almanac.

A note for the Southern Hemisphere

In Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and South America the seasons run the other way, so many witches there turn the Wheel round: Yule is kept at the June solstice and Litha in December, Samhain around 1 May and Beltane around 1 November. The astronomical moments are the same worldwide; only the local clock time and the meaning of the season change. This page is written for the UK and Ireland.

Questions

When is Yule 2027 in the UK?

The winter solstice of 2027 falls at 02:42 GMT on Wednesday 22 December. Most British witches mark Yule on the evening of Tuesday 21 December, the longest night, or on the 22nd itself. Some websites say 21 December because they use American time zones, where the solstice is still the evening of the 21st.

Is the Wheel of the Year ancient?

The festivals are old; the wheel is modern. Samhain, Imbolc, Beltane and Lughnasadh appear in early medieval Irish writing, and midsummer, midwinter and Lammas were kept in Britain for centuries. Gathering eight of them into one evenly spaced wheel happened in English modern Paganism in the 1950s and 1960s.

Why are there two dates for Imbolc, Beltane, Lammas and Samhain?

The traditional dates (1 February, 1 May, 1 August and 31 October to 1 November) are calendar dates. The astronomical dates, when the Sun is exactly halfway between a solstice and an equinox, come a few days later. Both are legitimate; many people celebrate on the traditional date.

When is the summer solstice 2027 in the UK?

At 15:10 BST on Monday 21 June 2027. In 2026 it fell at 09:24 BST on Sunday 21 June.
